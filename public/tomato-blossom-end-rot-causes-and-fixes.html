Tomato Blossom End Rot: Causes, Prevention & Organic Fixes

Why tomato bottoms turn black and leathery—and how to correct soil moisture and calcium uptake before you lose your harvest.

What is Blossom End Rot?

Few sights are more heartbreaking to a gardener than checking on beautiful, ripening tomatoes only to discover dark, water-soaked patches on the bottom (blossom end) of the fruit. As the fruit grows, this spot expands into a flattened, sunken, leathery black or brown lesion.

Key Fact: Blossom end rot is not caused by a fungus, bacteria, or virus. It is a physiological disorder caused by a localized lack of calcium within the developing fruit tissue. Spraying chemical fungicides or copper sprays will do nothing to treat or stop it.

According to horticultural research from University of Maryland Extension, blossom end rot also frequently strikes bell peppers, eggplants, squash, and watermelons.

The Real Cause: Why Calcium Fails to Reach Fruit

Most garden soils naturally contain sufficient calcium. The problem almost always lies in how the plant transports calcium from the soil into its fruits:

  • Moisture Inconsistency: Calcium moves through a plant exclusively via the transpiration stream (the flow of water absorbed by roots and evaporated through leaves). When soil swings between bone-dry and waterlogged, calcium uptake stops dead in its tracks.
  • Rapid Vegetative Growth from Excess Nitrogen: Using fertilizers high in fast-release nitrogen (such as 24-8-16) prompts the plant to produce massive amounts of dark green foliage. Leaves transpire faster than young fruit, diverting all available calcium to the leaves and starving the baby tomatoes.
  • Container Soil Drying: Potted tomatoes dry out significantly faster than plants in raised beds or ground soil. A single hot afternoon where soil dries out completely can cause blossom end rot on fruits forming that week.
  • Root Damage: Deep hoeing or aggressive cultivation too close to the tomato stem damages feeder roots responsible for nutrient absorption.

5 Proven Steps to Fix and Prevent Blossom End Rot

1. Establish Consistent, Deep Watering

Water deeply at the base of the tomato plant 2–3 times per week rather than shallow daily sprinkles. The goal is to keep soil evenly moist like a wrung-out sponge. In hot summer weather, container tomatoes may require watering twice daily.

2. Apply a 3-Inch Organic Mulch Layer

Spread clean straw, shredded hardwood bark, or chemical-free grass clippings across the soil surface around your tomato plants. Mulch conserves soil moisture, prevents evaporation, and stabilizes root temperatures during intense heat waves.

3. Switch to a Tomato-Specific Organic Fertilizer

Avoid high-nitrogen synthetic fertilizers once flowering begins. Switch to a fertilizer formulated specifically for tomatoes (e.g., 4-6-8 or 3-4-6) containing lower nitrogen, higher phosphorus and potassium, and naturally chelated calcium.

4. Test Soil pH Before Adding Lime

Tomatoes absorb calcium best in soil with a pH between 6.2 and 6.8. If your soil is acidic (below 6.0), adding agricultural dolomitic lime in the spring raises the pH and supplies calcium. If your soil pH is already above 6.5, do not add lime; use agricultural gypsum (calcium sulfate) instead, which provides calcium without altering pH.

5. Choose Larger Pots for Container Tomatoes

Determinate (bush) tomatoes need at least a 5-gallon container; indeterminate (vining) varieties require 10 to 15 gallons of soil volume. Larger soil reservoirs maintain stable moisture levels and prevent rapid drying.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you eat tomatoes with blossom end rot?

Yes. Because it is not a pathogen or microbial rot, the undamaged upper portion of the tomato is completely safe to eat. Simply slice off the leathery bottom and compost the damaged section.

Should I remove fruit already showing black bottoms?

Yes. Once a tomato exhibits blossom end rot, the fruit tissue cannot repair itself. Pluck affected tomatoes off the vine so the plant redirects its energy and calcium toward healthy new fruit.

Do calcium sprays on leaves work?

Foliar calcium sprays have very limited effectiveness. Calcium is immobile within plant tissue; it does not travel from leaves into fruit. Ensuring steady root hydration remains the only proven remedy.
